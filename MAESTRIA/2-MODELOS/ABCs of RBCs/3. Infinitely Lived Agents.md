A frequently used way to study **endogenous saving** is to assume that agents live forever and therefore take their whole future consumption path into account. One can think of each agent as a *family dynasty* whose currently-living members care about the welfare of descendants not yet born. The main technical advantage is that, when preferences and technology are time-invariant, we can look for **stationary states** using either variational or recursive methods.

When the expected date of death is sufficiently far away and **future utility is discounted** by a factor $\beta$ with $0<\beta<1$, consumption far past one’s expected lifetime receives negligible weight. If agents also value leaving bequests, their objective effectively has an infinite horizon. Moreover, modelling life as a sequence of many periods helps connect the framework to short-run phenomena like business cycles (typically < 10 years), whereas two-period overlapping-generations models make each “lifetime” too coarse for that purpose.

(The downside of infinitely-lived agents is the absence of a pure “retirement” decision; overlapping-generations models in Chapter 2 handle that instead.)

## 3.1 A Robinson Crusoe Economy with Fixed Labor

Consider an economy with a *single* infinitely-lived individual (a social planner). At date $t$ the agent maximises

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \beta^{i}\,u(c_{t+i}),
$$

where $u(\cdot)$ is increasing, concave, time-invariant, and twice differentiable; $\beta\in(0,1)$ is the discount factor; and $c_t$ denotes consumption in period $t$.

### Budget constraints  

The agent enters period $t$ with capital $k_t$. Output is produced with a production function $f(k_t)$ (labor is fixed at 1), and **capital accumulates** according to

$$
k_{t+1}=(1-\delta)k_t+i_t,
\qquad
y_t=f(k_t)=c_t+i_t,
$$

where $i_t$ is investment and $\delta\in(0,1)$ is the depreciation rate.

Combining the two constraints yields consumption as a function of capital:

$$
c_t = f(k_t) - k_{t+1} + (1-\delta)k_t. \tag{3.1}
$$

---

### 3.1.1 Variational methods

A convenient way to find a **stationary state** is to treat the entire infinite sequence $\{k_t\}_{t=0}^{\infty}$ as the choice variable, substitute \eqref{3.1} into utility, and maximise. Fixing $k_{s-1}$ and $k_{s+1}$, the first-order condition for $k_s$ (the *Euler equation*) is

$$
u'\!\left(f(k_s)-k_{s+1}+(1-\delta)k_s\right)
\;=\;
\beta\,u'\!\left(f(k_{s+1})-k_{s+2}+(1-\delta)k_{s+1}\right)
\,[\,f'(k_{s+1})+1-\delta\,].
$$

In a stationary state ($k_{s-1}=k_s=k_{s+1}=k$) this reduces to

$$
f'(k)=\frac{1}{\beta}-1+\delta,
$$

i.e. the marginal product of capital equals the **required real return** $(1/\beta)-1$ plus depreciation.

---

#### Transversality condition  

Optimality also requires  

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}\,
\beta^{t}\,u'(c_t)\,k_t = 0,
$$

ensuring that the discounted utility value of capital held “at infinity” is negligible; otherwise the agent would prefer to postpone consumption forever and no finite optimum would exist.

---

### An example where the transversality condition fails

Let $f(k)=0.2k$ and $u(c)=c$ (linear). Choose $\delta=0.1$ and $\beta=0.98$. If the agent lives only $T$ periods, the optimal plan is to set $c_t=0$ for $t<T$ and consume everything at $T$, giving utility

$$
\left[(0.98)(1.1)\right]^{T}\!k_0.
$$

As $T\to\infty$, utility diverges; consumption is delayed indefinitely, violating the transversality condition and showing that no stationary-state optimum exists—even though a continuum of *feasible* stationary allocations $c_t=0.1k_t$ do.

## 3.2 A Robinson Crusoe Economy with Variable Labor

Now allow the agent to choose leisure $\ell_t\in(0,1)$ each period; labor supplied is $h_t=1-\ell_t$. Lifetime utility is

$$
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t}\,u(c_t,h_t),
\quad
u_c>0,\;
u_h<0.
$$

Production uses capital and labor:

$$
y_t=f(k_t,h_t)=c_t+i_t,
\qquad
k_{t+1}=(1-\delta)k_t+i_t.
$$

The Lagrangian yields four first-order conditions; in a stationary state they collapse to three equations in $(k,c,h)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_h(c,h)&=-u_c(c,h)\,f_h(k,h),\\[4pt]
f'(k)+1-\delta&=\frac{1}{\beta},\\[4pt]
\delta k &= f(k,h)-c.
\end{aligned}
$$

---

### Cobb–Douglas example  

Take $f(k,h)=k^{\theta}h^{1-\theta}$ and  

$$
u(c,h)=\ln c + B\ln(1-h),
\qquad B>0.
$$

Solving the three stationary conditions gives closed-form expressions (constants $G$ and $J$ depend only on parameters):

$$
\boxed{h = G\,k^{\theta/(1-\theta)}},
\quad
\boxed{c = J\,k^{\theta}},
\quad
\boxed{k = 
\frac{(1-\theta)(\theta+J)}
      {G\bigl[BJ+(1-\theta)(\theta+J)\bigr]}}.
$$

(See Exercise 3.1 for the CES utility variant.)

## 3.3 A Competitive Economy

Replace the planner with a unit continuum of identical households and competitive firms. Each household maximises

$$
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^{t}\,u(c_t^i,h_t^i)
$$

subject to

$$
c_t^i = w_t h_t^i + r_t k_t^i - i_t^i,
\quad
k_{t+1}^i=(1-\delta)k_t^i+i_t^i,
$$

while firms hire labor and capital so that $w_t=f_h(K_t,H_t)$ and $r_t=f_k(K_t,H_t)$. Aggregating over identical agents ($K_t=k_t^i$, $H_t=h_t^i$) collapses the equilibrium conditions to the *same* aggregate Euler, intratemporal, and resource constraints found in the Robinson Crusoe economy, confirming the **Second Welfare Theorem** in this frictionless setting.

---

## 3.4 The Second Welfare Theorem (brief recap)

> *With convex preferences and technologies, and complete competitive markets, any Pareto-efficient allocation can be decentralised as a competitive equilibrium via suitable lump-sum transfers of wealth.*

Thus the planner’s solution found above coincides with a competitive equilibrium when all agents start with identical wealth.

### A distortion that breaks the equivalence  

Introduce a wage tax $\tau_t$ whose revenue is rebated lump-sum $T_t$ to households. The individual’s budget constraint becomes  

$$
c_t^i = (1-\tau_t)w_t h_t^i + r_t k_t^i + T_t - i_t^i,
$$

and one first-order condition changes to  

$$
\frac{u_h}{u_c} = -(1-\tau_t)f_h(K_t,H_t),
$$

shifting the stationary state away from the undistorted optimum—even though the government’s budget balances ($\tau_t w_t H_t=T_t$) and aggregate feasibility is unaffected.

A numerical example with Cobb–Douglas $f$ and log–log utility yields lower consumption $c$, capital $K$, labor $H$, and per-period utility $u$ as $\tau$ rises (see Table 3.1 in the original text).

## 3.5 Reprise  

Variational methods make it straightforward to obtain stationary states in representative-agent growth models, and—thanks to the Second Welfare Theorem—those stationary states often describe competitive equilibria as well. Care is needed, however, whenever taxes, market power, or other **distortions** violate the theorem’s assumptions.
