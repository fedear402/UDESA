## Why

- The Home Market Effect (HME) in Krugman's (1980) model of trade with monopolistic competition and increasing returns to scale refers to the idea that: A country with a larger domestic demand for a product will host a disproportionately large share of the industry producing that product and will become a net exporter of it.

- The HME has been characterized as one particularly novel finding to emerge from IRTS theories of trade.
  • In particular, in a CRTS world (with trade costs or not), we would not expect an increase in a country's relative demand for a good to cause that country to export more of the good.

## Costinot, Donaldson, Kyle, and Williams (2016)

- **Costinot, A., Donaldson, D., Kyle, M., & Williams, H. (2019). The more we die, the more we sell? a simple test of the home-market effect. The quarterly journal of economics, 134(2), 843-894.**

- Distinguish between **weak** and **strong** home market effects (HME):
  • **Weak HME**: Do countries with larger domestic markets sell more abroad? (Linder, 1961)
  • **Strong HME**: Does this lead to a positive link between net exports and home demand? (Krugman, 1980)

- Empirical challenge:
  • Joint determination of local demand and supply.
  • So correlation between home expenditure and foreign sales does not provide a test of the HME—want to isolate demand differences

- Solution:
  • use observable demand shifters (e.g., disease burdens)
  • Pharmaceuticals: Variation in demographic characteristics ⇒ Variation in disease burdens ⇒ Variation in demand for drugs

## Main findings

- The core of the empirical strategy is the observation that a country's exogenous demographic composition can be used as...

- ...a predictor of the diseases that its inhabitants are most likely to die from

- ...and, in turn, the drugs that they are most likely to demand.

- The correlation between predicted home demand and sales abroad is positive and greater than the correlation between predicted home demand and purchases from abroad.

- In short, countries tend to be net sellers of the drugs that they demand the most, as predicted by Linder (1961) and Krugman (1980).

## Model (I): Demand

Aggregate demand in country $j$ for drugs targeting disease $n$ (e.g. cardiovascular disease):

$$D_j^n = \theta_j^n D(P_j^n/P_j)D_j$$

Demand in country $j$ for varieties from country $i$ for disease $n$:

$$d_{ij}^n = d(p_{ij}^n/P_j^n)D_j^n$$

with country-disease price index implicitly defined by:

$$P_j^n = \sum_i p_{ij}^n d(p_{ij}^n/P_j^n)$$

## A Simple Test of the Home-Market Effect

- Consider first-order approximation around symmetric equilibrium (with identical trade costs, $\tau_{ij}^n = \tau$ for all $i \neq j$ and $n$)

- Then, bilateral foreign sales ($x_{ij}^n \equiv p_{ij}^n d_{ij}^n$, for any $i \neq j$) given by::

$$\ln x_{ij}^n = \delta_{ij} + \delta_n + \beta_M \ln \theta_j^n + \beta_X \ln \theta_i^n + \varepsilon_{ij}^n$$

  • $\delta_{ij}$: pair fixed effects
  • $\delta_n$: disease fixed effects
  • $\varepsilon_{ij}^n$: error term (all other variation in trade costs + supply conditions). Notice that it does not depend on $\theta$.

## Home-Market Effect Definitions

- Total exports: $X_i^n = \sum_{j \neq i} x_{ij}^n$
- Total imports: $M_i^n = \sum_{j \neq i} x_{ji}^n$
- Definition:

**Weak HME**: $\frac{d \ln X_i^n}{d \ln \theta_i^n} > 0 \Leftrightarrow \beta_X > 0$

- Do countries with larger domestic markets tend to sell relatively more in foreign markets? (Linder, 1961)

**Strong HME**: $\frac{d \ln X_i^n}{d \ln \theta_i^n} > \frac{d \ln M_i^n}{d \ln \theta_i^n} \Leftrightarrow \beta_X > \beta_M$

- Is this effect so strong as to create a positive relationship between net foreign sales of a good and its local demand? (Krugman, 1980)

## Data on Disease Burdens

- *The disease burden*: A proxy $\{\theta_i^n\}$: demographic shifter of demand.

- They exploit plausibly exogenous source of demand-side variation for each drug, in each country, by isolating the apparent extent to which drugs have a demographic bias in their relevance, as well as the extent to which countries differ in the demographic composition of their populations.

- Building on Acemoglu and Linn (2004): use demographic data to construct a predictor of country-disease demand. Draw on:

1. WHO Global Burden of Disease data: DALYs by age-gender-disease-country
2. Hand-coded crosswalk of about 600 ATC codes to 60 WHO diseases
   • e.g. A02B linked to peptic ulcer disease"
   • 60 WHO diseases are empirical counterpart of diseases n in our model
3. US Census International Database: demographic sizes

## Predicted Disease Burden (PDB)

$$\text{PDB}_i^n = \sum_{a,g} \left[\text{pop}_{iag} \cdot \left(\frac{\sum_{j \neq i} \text{disease burden}_{kag}^n}{\sum_{kag}^{j \neq i} \text{pop}_{kag}}\right)\right]$$

- The ratio measures the average disease burden per capita from disease n for gender g and age group a in 2012, calculated excluding the country of interest (that is, summing over all countries k except for country i).

- This ratio is then weighted by the population for that gender g and age group a, and summed across age and gender groups, for a given country i in 2012.

- a: age group
- g: gender

## Other Work on the HME

- Davis & Weinstein (JIE 2003): Clever way of nesting an HO and IRTS model

- Hanson & Xiang (AER 2004) Test more in spirit of Helpman-Krugman (1985) version of HME: do large countries specialize relatively more in high-IRTS industries?

- Head Ries (AER 2001) Study whether a country's share of production grows more or less than proportionally with increase in demand.

- Behrens et al. (JIE 2009) Point out that extending Helpman-Krugman (1985) from 2 to N countries is hard, and that the simple HME doesn't survive.
