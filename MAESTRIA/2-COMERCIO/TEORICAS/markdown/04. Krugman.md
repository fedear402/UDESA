## New Trade Theory

- **Neoclassical (old) trade theory**: Ricardian model trade motivated by technology, Heckscher-Ohlin model by factor endowment

- These models are at odds with the following evidence:
  • Large part of trade occurs **between similar countries** (50%) and a large fraction of total trade takes place intra-industry (60%)
  • misses important aspects of the effects of trade liberalization → **within industry** reallocations
  • hard to think about **firms** (intra-firm trade, multinational activity).

- **New trade theory**
  • **scale economies**
  • **imperfect competition** and **product differentiation**

## New Trade Theory

- Balassa (1967) proposed **monopolistic competition** as a setup for explaining the observed trade flows within the European common market.¹

- These types of models gained prominence in the 1980s, because they can explain **trade between similar countries** and **intra-industry trade**:
  • even within industries firms may produce **different varieties** of the industry's product;
  • if consumers value "variety" or tastes over varieties are heterogeneous, many brands are consumed;

- **Basic idea**:
  • increasing returns provide incentives for an individual variety to **concentrate in one location**
  • contries specialize in different goods to exploit advantage of large scale production

¹Bela Balassa, "Trade creation and trade diversion in the European Common Market", *Economic Journal*, March 1967.

## Monopolistic competition: basics

- With internal economies of scale (IES) there cannot be perfect competition:
  • IES: unit cost of a firm declines with quantity produced
  • External ES: unit cost of a firm declines with total quantity produced by the sector/economy

- Chamberlain (1933) and Joan Robinson (1933) introduced monopolistic competition:²
  • Every firm has some **market power** in the particular variety of the good she produces → firms are price setters (face a downward sloping demand curve)
  • There is a **large number of firms** ⟹ a price change by one firm has no effect on the demand faced by the other firms
  • There is **free entry** ⟹ firms' profits are driven down to zero

- With IES a firm has an incentive to differentiate its brand from other firm and earn monopoly power on that brand

²Edward Chamberlin (1933), *The theory of monopolistic competition*, Joan Robinson (1933), *The economics of imperfect competition*.

## Monopolistic competition: a short review

- Goods are not homogeneous, but **differentiated**: Varieties of a good, differentiated by their quality, for example, or marketing (e.g. apple vs. other computers)

- Differentiation allows a firm to influence the price of the good (**imperfect competition**)

- Market structure: many producers, each producing under **increasing returns to scale (IRS)** a different variety, but competing between them for consumers' spending³

- IRS emerge when average costs fall the more output is produced, which pushes towards specialisation in the most successful varieties

- Because of IRS, exporting is a way to increase output sales, and further reduce average costs... despite of countries being similar in terms of technologies (Ricardo) or factor endowments (HO)

³Increasing return to scale is the general term, in our context we know that returns to scale are internal to the firm.

## Monopolistic competition: basics

- Profits are positive, if entry is possible (no barriers) firms will enter

- If goods are homogeneous, a firm will enter at a lower price and get all the market

- If goods are differentiated, firm entry flattens demand for incumbents (price increase come at the price of larger drop in demand)

- IRS ⟹ average costs lie above marginal costs (AC > MC). Ex. because there is a fixed cost F to set up the firm and start selling:

$$AC = \frac{C}{Q} = \frac{F + cQ}{Q} = \frac{F}{Q} + c > c = MC$$

⟹ goods produced only if firms can charge a price above MC (imperfect competition)

- Free entry and exit from the industry ⟹ profits are zero in the long run

## Environment

- **Preferences**. Constant elasticity of substitution (CES) preferences over a continuum of varieties $\omega \in (0, n)$

$$U = \left(\int_0^n q(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} d\omega\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}} \quad \text{(Utility)}$$

with $\sigma > 1$ elasticity of substitution between varieties⁴

$$l(q) = f + \frac{q}{\phi} \quad \text{(technology)}$$

  • technology homogeneous across varieties
  • Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS) modelled as fixed cost $f$

- **Market structure**: monopolistic competition

## Equilibrium closed economy: households

- **Households**.

$$\max_{q(\omega)} U = \left(\int_0^n q(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} d\omega\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}}$$

$$s.t. \int_0^n p(\omega)q(\omega)d\omega = wL = E$$

which yields

$$q(\omega) = \left(\frac{p(\omega)}{P}\right)^{-\sigma} \frac{wL}{P} \quad (1)$$

with

$$P = \left(\int_0^n p(\omega)^{1-\sigma}d\omega\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma}} \quad (2)$$

where $P$ is the **price index**, the minimum cost of 1 unit of utility $U$.

## Derivation of demand function

Write Lagrangian

$$L = \left(\int_0^n q(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} d\omega\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}} + \mu \left(wL - \int_0^n p(\omega)q(\omega)d\omega\right)$$

first order condition (FOC) with respect to (w.r.t.) any $q(\omega)$ yields

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial q(\omega)} = \frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1} \left(\int_0^n q(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} d\omega\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}-1} \frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma} q(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}-1} = \mu p(\omega)$$

take FOC for any two $q(\omega)$ and $q(\omega')$ and divide them

$$\left(\frac{q(\omega)}{q(\omega')}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}} = \frac{p(\omega)}{p(\omega')}$$

solve w.r.t. $q(\omega)$ and sub into budget constraint yields

$$q(\omega') = \frac{p(\omega')^{-\sigma}}{\int_0^n p(\omega)^{1-\sigma}} wL$$

## Equilibrium closed economy: households

Deriving the price index: from FOC we get

$$\frac{q(\omega)}{q(\omega')} = \left(\frac{p(\omega)}{p(\omega')}\right)^{-\sigma}$$

Let $P$ denote the price index, the price of the consumption index $U$. This is defined such that:

$$\left(\frac{q(\omega)}{U}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}} = \frac{p(\omega)}{P} \text{ for any } \omega.$$

Solve for $q(\omega)$

$$U = \left(\int_0^n \left(\left(\frac{p(\omega)}{P}\right)^{-\sigma} U\right)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} d\omega\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}}$$

substitute into the utility function, rearrange and get (2)

## Production

- Firms problem is

$$\max_{p(\omega)} \pi(\omega) = p(\omega)q(\omega) - w\left(f + \frac{q(\omega)}{\phi}\right)$$

$$s.t. q(\omega) = \left(\frac{p(\omega)}{P}\right)^{-\sigma} \frac{wL}{P}$$

- leads to markup pricing:⁵

$$p(\omega) = \frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1} \frac{w}{\phi} = p \quad (3)$$

⁵Note: in FOC price decision of a firm does not affect the price index. Each firm is monopolist in their variety but cannot influence the aggregate price. So each firm has no market power with respect to the entire economy.

## Free entry

- **Free entry**. Basic idea of monopolistic competition is that firms can set the price (they have market power) but new firms (varieties) will enter till there are no more profits

$$\pi(\omega) = p(\omega)q(\omega) - w\left(f + \frac{q(\omega)}{\phi}\right) = 0$$

using (3) we obtain

$$q(\omega) = (\sigma-1)\phi f = q \quad \text{(FE)}$$

Symmetric model → same price and same quantity for all firms

*Remark 1*. Entry is intended as new varieties entering the market, entry will stop at $\pi(\omega) = 0$ and the equilibrium number of firms $n$ will be determined (see below)

*Remark 2*. With increasing returns, no incentives to duplicate a variety, since the entrant's cost will be higher than incumbent's

## Optimal production

- Cost linear in $q(\omega)$, domestic and export production problems **separable**⁶

- Domestic prices same as before

$$p(\omega) = \frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1} \frac{w}{\phi} = p \quad (4)$$

- Export optimal price

$$\max_{p_x(\omega)} \pi_x(\omega) = p_x(\omega)q_x(\omega) - w\left(f + \frac{\tau q_x(\omega)}{\phi}\right)$$

$$s.t. q_x(\omega) = \left(\frac{p_x(\omega)}{P^*}\right)^{-\sigma} \frac{w^*L^*}{P^*}$$

- leads to markup pricing:

$$p_x(\omega) = \frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1} \frac{\tau w}{\phi} = \tau p \quad (5)$$

⁶Can be solved separately.

## Gains from trade I: autarky to trade

- **Result 1**. *Going from autarky to costly trade increases the number of varieties consumed (from $n$ to $n + n^*$) and therefore increases welfare*

- **Proof**. Compute price index under trade $P_T$ using equilibrium prices and $n$

$$P_T^{1-\sigma} = np^{1-\sigma} + n^*(\tau p^*)^{1-\sigma}$$

in closed economy we have $P^{1-\sigma} = np^{1-\sigma}$, presence of foreign variety ⟹ $P_T < P$

- Assume $w = 1$ (numeraire), Home welfare larger under trade

$$U_T = w_T/P_T > U_A = w_A/P_A$$

- Source of gains from trade → **love for variety**

## Love for variety

- Utility function features **love for variety** which is a consumption externality: consumers benefit from a wider and diverse set of consumption options

- From (6), demand and quantity sold do not vary with trade. For simplicity let's call this constant quantity $q(\omega) = (\sigma-1)f\phi = \bar{q}$, sub into utility

$$U = \left(\int_0^{n+n^*} q(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} d\omega\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}} = (n + n^*)^{\frac{\sigma}{(\sigma-1)}} \bar{q}$$

welfare is increasing in the number of goods consumed

- **Love-for-variety in production**: Assume that there is a unique final consumption good produced with a large variety of intermediates⁷

$$Y = \left(\int_0^{n+n^*} k(\omega)^{\frac{\sigma-1}{\sigma}} d\omega\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma-1}} = (n + n^*)^{\frac{\sigma}{(\sigma-1)}} \bar{k}$$

large set of intermediate goods (e.g. machines), more efficient production

gains from trade from improved efficiency via love-for-variety in production

⁷This is an alternative modelling option giving you exactly the same results.

## Intensive and extensive margin

- **Remark**. From (6) we see that going from autarky to trade does not affect the total quantity sold $q$ but only the number of varieties available (**extensive margin**) and the share of total quantity exported. This is due to the assumption of constant elasticity of substitution

- Krugman (1979) assumes that the elasticity increases with the number of varieties available. Trade openness leads to increase in the quantity produced (**intensive margin**).

## Gains from trade II: Incremental liberalization

- **Result 2**. *Trade liberalization ($\downarrow \tau$) does not affect number of varieties ($n$ and $n^*$ pinned down by population) but reduces price of imported goods, increasing consumption of imported varieties*

- **Proof**. Computing the two price indexes using equilibrium prices and number of firms

$$P_T^{1-\sigma} = np^{1-\sigma} + n^*(\tau p^*)^{1-\sigma} \quad (8)$$
$$= \lambda \left(Lw^{1-\sigma} + L^*\tau^{1-\sigma}\right)$$

and

$$P_T^{*1-\sigma} = n(\tau p)^{1-\sigma} + n^*p^{*1-\sigma} \quad (9)$$
$$= \lambda \left[L(\tau w)^{1-\sigma} + L^*\right]$$

we set $w^* = 1$ (note we're changing numeraire) and $\lambda = \frac{1}{\sigma f}\left[\frac{\sigma}{(\sigma-1)\phi}\right]^{1-\sigma}$

- **Note**: total quantity produced does not change $q_t = q_d + \tau q_x$ but quantity exported increases $q_x = \left(\frac{\tau p}{P^*}\right)^{-\sigma} \frac{w^*L^*}{P^*}$

- Firms reduce domestic production and increase production for export mkt.

## Gains from trade II: Incremental liberalization

- Home welfare is

$$U_T = \frac{w}{P} = w\lambda^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}} \left[L\left(\frac{1}{w}\right)^{\sigma-1} + \frac{L^*}{\tau^{\sigma-1}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}$$

thus $\partial U/\partial \tau < 0$, not that fast.... we need to take into account the wage adjustment

- Symmetric countries, $L = L^*$, $w = w^* = 1$:

$$U_T = \frac{w}{P} = \lambda^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}} L^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}} \left[1 + \tau^{1-\sigma}\right]^{\frac{1}{\sigma-1}}$$

hence $\partial U/\partial \tau < 0$

- **Intuition**. Welfare improves only because of lower foreign prices → no variety effect!

- Source of welfare gains is still love for variety: if goods become perfect substitutes ($\sigma \to \infty$) ⇒ $U = 1$!
## Gains from trade II: Incremental liberalization

- **Scenario 1**, Autarky to costly trade: only **extensive margin** operative

- **Sceario 2**: Incremental trade liberalization: does not affect number of varieties traded ($n + n^*$) but increases the quantity consumed of imported varieties (since export prices go down) → trade liberalization only increases export but does not change each firm total sales **intensive margin**

  • trade operates via an intensive margin but only by reshuffling sales from domestic to export
  • a proper intensive margin (total quantity change) is not operative

- Empirical evidence shows that both margins are operative when you reduce trade costs: can we reconcile theory with evidence?
