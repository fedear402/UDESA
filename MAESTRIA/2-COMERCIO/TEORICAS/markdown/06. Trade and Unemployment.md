→ Historia estilo Hecksher-Ohlin:
→ país abundante en empleados high-skilled →, aumenta $w^H/w^L$.

- No hay libre movilidad del trabajo a través de las regiones/industrias: hay costos de ajuste del comercio en corto plazo.
→ Ejemplo: Liberas el comercio → el sector textil se hace más pequeño. Que pasa con los trabajadores de dicho sector? Que dice Hecksher-Ohlin sobre esto?
1. H-O: supone libre movilidad de trabajadores.
2. En realidad, puede pasar que los trabajadores textiles no puedan trabajar en "software". ↑ Aumenta el desempleo.

## Efecto de Comercio en salario relativo de High Skilled versus Low skilled

- Evidencia en países desarrollados y en vías de desarrollo de aumento de este ratio.
- Sugerente de que aumenta la desigualdad.
- Mecanismos?

1. Explicación estilo H-O: Un país desarrollado es abundante en mano de obra high-skilled. Se abre al comercio y se especializa en sectores que demandan high-skilled. Mejora el salario de esos trabajadores.

2. Pero por que se ve esto también en países en vías de desarrollo?

3. Comercio beneficia a las empresas más grandes o de mejor calidad, que típicamente contratan mano de obra más high skilled. Por lo tanto, esta es otra vía por la que el comercio aumenta los salarios de los high-skilled en detrimento de los low skilled.

- Es esto suficiente para estar en contra al comercio? Sabemos que mejora la productividad y el crecimiento.
- Recomendación comúnmente aceptada: apertura al comercio con políticas de corto plazo para re-distribuir.

## Empirical Strategy

- Identify the causal impact of Chinese import competition on U.S. local labor markets.

- **Strategy:**
  • Use variation in import exposure by industry and location.
  • Also: Instrument for U.S. imports from China with Chinese exports to other high-income countries.

---

## Constructing exposure of a region to China shock

- Exposure of a region to China shock: based on initial manufacturing shares. Formally:

$$\Delta IPW_{uit} = \sum_j \frac{L_{ijt-1}}{L_{ujt-1}} \times \frac{\Delta M_{ucjt}}{L_{it-1}}$$

- where i: region, j: industry, t: year.
- $L_{it}$: Employment at t in region i.
- $L_{ijt}$: Employment at t in industry j in region i.
- $L_{ujt}$: Total Employment at t in industry j in the US.
- $\frac{L_{ijt-1}}{L_{ujt-1}}$: Share of region i in US employment in industry j.
- $\Delta M_{ucjt}$: Change in US imports from China in industry j between the start and end of the period.
- Idea: the difference in the shock across local labor markets stems entirely from variation in local industry employment structure before the shocks.
- A region i will be more exposed if high share in affected industries.

---

## Constructing of Bartik Instrument

- $\Delta M_{ucjt}$ might depend on demand factors in the US. e.g:
- **Instrumental Variable:** Predicted import exposure based on China's exports to other high-income countries.

- **Formula:**
$$\Delta IPW_{oit} = \sum_j \frac{L_{ijt-1}}{L_{ujt-1}} \times \frac{\Delta M_{ocjt}}{L_{it-1}}$$

- **Purpose:** To isolate the supply-driven component of import growth from demand-driven factors.

## Overview

In theory, trade generates aggregate gains through:
- Lower prices and increased product variety (Broda and Weinstein, 2006)
- Cheaper and more diverse inputs for firms (Goldberg et al., 2010)
- Stimulus to innovation (Bloom, Draca, and Van Reenen, 2011)

Yet, distributional consequences of trade, especially losses in import-exposed areas, are critical.

---

## Transfers and Deadweight Loss

- For each $1,000 rise in import exposure per worker, annual transfers increase by $58 per capita.
- By multiplying $58 by the observed growth of exposure to Chinese imports and the fraction of this growth that we attribute to supply shocks:
- The annual transfer receipts increases:
  1990–2000: $58 \times 0.55 \approx 32$
  2000–2007: $58 \times 0.88 \approx 51$
- **Total:** $83 per capita

---

## Why Does DWL = MEB × Transfers?

**Marginal Excess Burden (MEB)** is the extra cost to society of raising an additional dollar through taxation, due to tax distortions (e.g., discouraging work or saving).

If MEB = 0.4 (as estimated in Gruber's (2010)), then raising $1 in taxes costs $1.40:
- $1 funds the transfer
- $0.40 is lost due to distortions — **deadweight loss (DWL)**

Therefore:
$$\text{DWL} = 0.4 \times 83 = \$33.2 \text{ per capita}$$

This is the **efficiency cost of taxation** used to fund trade-related transfers. Confidence interval: $22–$44 per capita.

## Deadweight Loss from Involuntary Unemployment

- Another source of inefficiency steams from the Involuntary labor force exits
- These cause deadweight loss when displaced workers' wages exceed their value of leisure.
- This loss is benchmarked by comparing their market wage to the value of leisure, with the gap representing the efficiency loss.

---

## Deadweight Loss from Involuntary Unemployment

- **Marginal condition:**
$$w_0u_c(y + w_0h_0, h_0) = -u_h(y + w_0h_0, h_0)$$

- The left side of the equation represents the extra utility from consuming what one earns in an hour of work at the optimal wage and hours. The right side shows the extra disutility or cost of working that hour.
- Assuming $u_c$ constant at initial wage, taking logs and differentiating yields:
$$\frac{\partial \ln w}{\partial \ln h} = \frac{1}{\eta_h}$$

- $\eta_h$ is the compensated labor supply elasticity: how much a person adjusts their hours worked in response to changes in wages, holding utility constant—that is, isolating the substitution effect and removing the income effect.

---

## Worker Surplus from Employment

To estimate a worker's **surplus from employment**, we compare:
- **Total labor earnings:** $w_0h_0$
- **Disutility of work:** obtained by integrating the labor supply function

The surplus is the difference between earnings and the value of leisure forgone:
$$\Delta = w_0h_0 - \frac{w_0h_0}{1 + 1/\eta_h} = \frac{w_0h_0}{\eta_h + 1}$$

**Interpretation:**
- $\eta_h$ is the compensated labor supply elasticity.
- The formula captures the net benefit a worker gains from employment, beyond compensation for the disutility of work.
- A higher elasticity implies a smaller surplus since the worker is more responsive to changes in wages.

---

## Estimating Deadweight Loss from Involuntary Unemployment

A trade shock may reduce both employment and wages, causing some workers to be involuntarily displaced.
- Part of the employment decline is voluntary—due to lower wages reducing labor supply.
- To isolate involuntary unemployment, we net out voluntary responses using labor supply elasticities from Chetty (2012):
  • Extensive margin (participation): $\eta_e \approx 0.25$
  • Intensive margin (hours): $\eta_h \approx 0.50$
- A 1,000 import shock reduces:
  • Wages by $\hat{\beta}_w = -0.76\%$
  • Participation by $\hat{\beta}_e = -0.77$ p.p.
- The observed participation drop is about 4 times larger than the predicted voluntary drop ($0.76\% \times 0.25 = 0.19\%$)

**Conclusion:** Approximately 75% of the employment decline is involuntary and contributes to deadweight loss.

---

## Welfare Loss from Lower Wages and Reduced Hours

Trade shocks reduce wages for remaining workers, leading to lower desired hours and a decline in worker surplus.

**Effect on Hours:**
$$w'_0 < w_0 \Rightarrow h'_0 \approx h_0\left(1 + \eta_h \ln\left(\frac{w'_0}{w_0}\right)\right)$$

**Let:** $\alpha = \frac{w'_0}{w_0}$ and approximate $\ln\left(\frac{w'_0}{w_0}\right) \approx \alpha - 1 \approx \hat{\beta}_w \cdot \Delta IPW_{ut}$

**Welfare Loss from Involuntary Employment:**
$$\Delta' = \alpha w_0h_0\left[\frac{1}{\eta_h + 1} + \frac{\eta_h}{\eta_h + 1}(\alpha - 1)\right] \quad (1)$$

**Interpretation:**
- This loss is smaller than the original worker surplus due to reduced wages and hours.
- Even employed workers experience a drop in surplus because of worsening labor market conditions.

## Deadweight Loss from Involuntary Unemployment

Using estimated responses to rising Chinese import exposure, the authors quantify welfare losses from involuntary labor market exits.

**Employment Effects:**
- Involuntary unemployment/nonparticipation increased by:
  • 0.32 p.p. (1990–2000) → earnings loss: $65 per capita
  • 0.52 p.p. (2000–2007) → earnings loss: $106 per capita

**Deadweight Loss Estimates (via Equation 1)**
- $43 per capita (1990–2000)
- $69 per capita (2000–2007)
- **Total:** $112 per capita
- **Range (±1 S.E.):** $87–$137

**Interpretation:**
- These medium-run losses may offset part of the gains from trade with China.

---

## Conclusion

Trade creates gains, but adjustment costs — such as:
- Government transfers, and
- Involuntary non-participation in the labour market

can introduce **sizable deadweight losses** that partly offset those gains.

These losses may diminish over time, but they are **non-negligible in the medium run**.

---

## Policy Implications

- Suggested policies: Job retraining, trade adjustment assistance, and targeted economic development.

- Importance of addressing regional disparities in trade exposure.

- General aspect: in the "short-run" there are adjustment costs. No free labor mobility between sectors (AND regions!) within a country.
