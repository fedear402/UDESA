- Eli Heckscher (student) and Bertil Ohlin (professor) at SSE
- The book: *Interregional and International Trade*, 1933 written only by Ohlin but work credited to Heckscher as well as a lot of the material came from his phd dissertation

## Heckscher-Ohlin great idea

- Comparative advantage based on differences in factor proportions:
  - cross-country differences in *relative factor endowments*
  - cross-industry differences in *relative factor intensities* → comparative advantage
- Second pillar of Classical trade theory¹ which focuses on **inter-industry trade**:
  - explain trade btw. labor-abundant Bangladesh and capital-abundant US
  - not useful to explain trade between similar advanced countries (e.g. UK-US)
- Useful to study **distributional effects** across different factors of production:
  - does trade affect wages and returns to capital differently?
  - does trade affect the wage of educated and non-educated workers differently?

¹The first is Ricardian model.

## The two-good, two factor model: technology

- Two goods: $i = 1, 2$
- Two factors: capital $K$ and labor $L$
- **Constant returns to scale technology** (CRS) technology²

$$y_i = f_i(K_i, L_i) \quad i = 1, 2$$

- This class of production functions are assumed to be **increasing, concave, and homogeneous of degree one** in the inputs $K_i$ and $L_i$ (constant returns to scale)
- Labor and capital **fully mobile** across industries
- Assume **small open economy**: goods prices are exogenously determined by world market → no need to model demand (consumption)
  - hence this is a 'partial equilibrium' version of the model
- All markets are **perfectly competitive**

²If you double $K$ and $L$ you double $y$.

---

## Equilibrium factor prices and production

- **Cost function approach** (for expositional purpose)³
- **Unit cost function**

$$c_i(w,r; y_i=1) = \min_{L_i,K_i} \{wL_i + rK_i\}$$

$$s.t. \quad y_i = f_i(K_i, L_i)$$

- **By Shepard's lemma** (envelope theorem)

$$\frac{\partial c_i}{\partial w} = a_{iL}, \quad \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial r} = a_{iK}$$

$a_{iL}$ is optimal labor demand in good $i$ (or unit labor requirement for that good)

³Recall that you can solve the firm problem (optimal labor and capital demand) with either profit maximization or cost minimization. The two approaches yield identical results.

---

## Equilibrium factor prices and production

- **Perfect competition implies**⁴

$$p_1 = c_1(w,r, 1) \quad (1)$$
$$p_2 = c_2(w,r, 1)$$

- **Factor market clearing conditions** close the model

$$a_{1L}y_1 + a_{2L}y_2 = L \quad (2)$$
$$a_{1K}y_1 + a_{2K}y_2 = K$$

- 4 equations (1)-(2) and 4 unknowns $(w,r, y_1, y_2)$
- **Remark 1**: recall $a_{iL}$ and $a_{iK}$ are functions of factor prices $w$ and $r$
- **Remark 2**: small open economy $\Rightarrow$ $p_1$ and $p_2$ exogenously given

⁴With CRS technology, unit costs are equal to both marginal and average costs. Check: derive the unit cost with a Cobb-Douglas technology, $y_i = L_i^{\alpha} K_i^{1-\alpha}$.

## Factor price insensitivity

If the is a unique solution to (1), we could solve equilibrium systems in blocks:
- get $w$ and $r$ from (1) then
- sub into (2) to get optimal production $y_1$ and $y_2$

### Lemma
*If both goods are produced and there is no Factor Intensity Reversal (FIR), then at each price vector $(p_1, p_2)$ corresponds a unique vector of factor prices $(w,r)$.*

- For a given vector of goods prices we can uniquely determine factor prices
  - factor prices are independent from factor endowments!
  - Think of factor prices with perfect competition and CRS

## Factor price insensitivity

- Assume **good 1 is L-intensive**: $a_{1L}/a_{1K} > a_{2L}/a_{2K}$
- Iso-cost curves are $p_i = c_i(w,r)$: at A equilibrium $w$ and $r$ for given $p_1$ and $p_2$

## Factor price equalization theorem (Samuelson, 1949)

- Ohlin's book was quite verbose and many of its insights were formalised mathematically by others later on
- First great insight is that of factor price equalization⁵

### Theorem
*Two countries with same technologies but different factor endowments engaged in **free trade** have same factor prices if there is **no FIR***

- Another necessary condition is that if **both goods are produced** but this is technical and we will assume it holds.
- Free trade means a unique relative price in the world market
- **Trade in goods perfect substitute for trade in factors of production!**
  - Closed economy: L-abundant country pays lower wage
  - Open economy: L-abundant country pays same wage of K-abundant because can produce more of L-intensive good and export it
- Policy implications: trade agreement promote cross-country convergence in factors incomes

⁵Samuelson, Paul A. "International Factor-Price Equalisation Once Again." The Economic Journal, vol. 59, no. 234, [Royal Economic Society, Wiley], 1949.

## Stolper-Samuelson theorem (1941)

A second key insight is on the link between changes in good and factor prices, formalised in Stolper and Samuelson (1941)

### Theorem
*An increase in the relative price of a good increases the real price of the factor used intensively in that good and decrease the real price of the other factor*

Totally differentiating the price conditions (1):

$$dp_i = a_{iL}dw + a_{iK}dr \Rightarrow \frac{dp_i}{p_i} = \frac{wa_{iL}}{c_i} \frac{dw}{w} + \frac{ra_{iK}}{c_i} \frac{dr}{r} \quad i = 1, 2$$

which can be rewritten in matrix form

$$\begin{pmatrix} \hat{p}_1 \\ \hat{p}_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \theta_{1L} & \theta_{1K} \\ \theta_{2L} & \theta_{2K} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \hat{w} \\ \hat{r} \end{pmatrix} \quad (3)$$

$\theta_{iL}$ and $\theta_{iK}$ are **cost share** of labor and capital in good $i$ (with $\theta_{iL} + \theta_{iK} = 1$), hats are percentage changes (e.g. $\hat{p}_i = dp_i/p_i$)

To proof the theorem, we need to invert the matrix of cost shares....

---

## Stolper-Samuelson theorem

The determinant of matrix $\theta$ is

$$|\theta| = \theta_{1L}\theta_{2K} - \theta_{1K}\theta_{2L} = \theta_{1L} - \theta_{2L} = \theta_{2K} - \theta_{1K}$$

I used $\theta_{iL} + \theta_{iK} = 1$

**good 1 is L-intensive**: $\Rightarrow \theta_{1L} - \theta_{2L} > 0$

proof: if $\frac{wa_{1L}}{c_1} > \frac{wa_{2L}}{c_2}$ and $\frac{ra_{1K}}{c_1} < \frac{ra_{2K}}{c_2} \Rightarrow a_{1L}/a_{1K} > a_{2L}/a_{2K}$, true by assumption

From (3) we get

$$\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 = (\theta_{1L} - \theta_{2L}) \hat{w} + (\theta_{1K} - \theta_{2K})\hat{r}$$

using $|\theta| = \theta_{1L} - \theta_{2L} = \theta_{2K} - \theta_{1K} > 0$ we get

$$\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 = (\theta_{1L} - \theta_{2L}) (\hat{w} - \hat{r})$$

An increase in relative price of good 1, $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 > 0$

increases relative price of labor, $\hat{w} - \hat{r} > 0$

---

## Stolper Samuelson theorem

Solving (3) w.r.t. factor prices we get (I'm using Cramer's rule)

$$\hat{w} = \frac{\theta_{2K}\hat{p}_1 - \theta_{1K}\hat{p}_2}{|\theta|} = \frac{(\theta_{2K} - \theta_{1K}) \hat{p}_1 + \theta_{1K} (\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2)}{\theta_{2K} - \theta_{1K}} > \hat{p}_1$$

since $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 > 0$⁶

Similarly we can show

$$\hat{r} = \frac{\theta_{1L}\hat{p}_2 - \theta_{2L}\hat{p}_1}{|\theta|} < \hat{p}_2$$

An increase in relative price of L-int. good, $\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 > 0$
- increases the real wage $(w/p_1, w/p_2)$
- decreases the real return to capital decreases $(r/p_1, r/p_2)$

Strong **distributional effects** of changes in relative prices

⁶Second equality obtained adding and subtracting $\theta_{1K}\hat{p}_1$.

## Changes in Endowments: Rybczynski (1955) theorem

The third key insight links factor endowments to production, formalised in Rybczynski (1955)⁷

### Theorem
*An increase in a factor's endowment increases the production of the good intensive of that factor and reduces the production of the other*

Totally differentiating the market clearing conditions (2) :

$$a_{1L}dy_1 + a_{2L}dy_2 = dL$$
$$a_{1K}dy_1 + a_{2K}dy_2 = dK$$

following same procedure as before get matrix form

$$\begin{pmatrix} \hat{L} \\ \hat{K} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_{1L} & \lambda_{2L} \\ \lambda_{1K} & \lambda_{2K} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \hat{y}_1 \\ \hat{y}_2 \end{pmatrix}$$

where $\lambda_{iL} = y_ia_{iL}/L$ is the fraction of labor force employed in good $i$

⁷Rybczynski, T. M. (1955). "Factor Endowment and Relative Commodity Prices". Economica. 22 (88): 336–341.

---

## Changes in Endowments: Rybczynski (1955) theorem

Assuming that $\hat{L} > 0$ and $\hat{K} = 0$ and proceeding as before we can show that

$$\hat{y}_1 = \frac{\lambda_{2K}}{\lambda_{2K} - \lambda_{2L}} \hat{L} > 0 \text{ and } \hat{y}_2 = \frac{-\lambda_{1K}}{\lambda_{2K} - \lambda_{2L}} \hat{L} < 0$$

if $L$ increases, L-intensive sector's production $y_1$ increases, while $y_2$ decreases

Opposite happens when the endowment of $K$ increases

we have now proved the key features of the model

## Taking stock

- A small open economy, with 2 goods and 2 factors
- CRS technology with different factor intensity
- We have shown:
  1. Factor price equalization (Samuelson, 1949)
  2. An increase in rel. price of a good increases the real price of the factor used intensively in that good and decreases the other (Stolper-Samuelson, 1941)
  3. An increase in a factor's endowment increases the production of the good intensive of that factor and reduces the production of the other (Rybczynski, 1955)

To explore the implications of trade we need to remove the small open economy assumption $\Rightarrow$ goods prices endogenously determined

## 2 goods 2 factors 2 country (2x2x2): the Heckscher-Ohlin model

- Two countries, Home (H), Foreign (F): **H is L-abundant**: $L = L^*$, and $K^* > K \rightarrow L/K > L^*/K^*$
- Technologies are identical across countries and CRS (as above)
- All markets are perfectly competitive
- **Good 1 is L-intensive**, good 2 is K-intensive
- Preferences are identical across countries and homothetic
- Trade is free → no trade costs!
- Factors move freely across sectors but not across countries → no migration, no capital flows!
- **General equilibrium (GE)**: remove assumption of small open economy, **relative prices endogenous**

---

## The production possibility frontier (PPF)

- We know how to get production, $y_1, y_2$ and factor prices $w,r$ given good prices
- Goal: find world good prices, and production for both countries
- To simplify the analysis we present a simpler way obtain $y_1, y_2$ given good prices
  - it's a different way to solve the firm problem without factor prices
  - we know how to determine factor prices once we have good prices (see above)

**Production possibility frontier (PPF)**: max $y_2$ for a given $y_1$ and resource constraints

$$\max_{K_2,L_2} y_2 = f_2(K_2, L_2)$$

$$s.t. \quad y_1 = f_1(K_1, L_1)$$
$$L_1 + L_2 = L$$
$$K_1 + K_2 = K$$

we get
$$y_2 = h(y_1, K, L)$$

## Production

Given goods prices the firm solves:

$$\max_{y_1,y_2} p_1y_1 + p_2y_2$$

$$s.t. \quad y_2 = h(y_1, K, L)$$

yielding

$$p = \frac{p_1}{p_2} = -\frac{\partial h}{\partial y_1} = -\frac{\partial y_2}{\partial y_1}$$

$\Rightarrow$ **relative price equal marginal rate of transformation** (slope of PPF)

optimal production mix: tangency between relative price and PPF

---

## Equilibrium for one country

Focus on H country: firm solve

$$\max_{y_1,y_2} p_1y_1 + p_2y_2$$

$$s.t. \quad y_2 = h(y_1, K, L)$$

yielding

$$p = \frac{p_1}{p_2} = -\frac{\partial h}{\partial y_1} = -\frac{\partial y_2}{\partial y_1}$$

Consumers solve ($c_1$ and $c_2$ is consumption of 1 and 2)

$$\max_{c_1,c_2} U(c_1, c_2)$$

$$s.t. \quad p_1c_1 + p_2c_2 = wL + rK = E$$

yielding: **relative price equal marginal rate of substitution**

$$p = \frac{p_1}{p_2} = -\frac{U_2}{U_1}$$

Markets clear: $c_1 = y_1$ and $c_2 = y_2$

---

## Autarky equilibrium

[Figure showing PPF curves for both countries with price lines]

Recall, H is L-abundant and 1 is L-intensive → 1 must be relatively cheaper in H

Equilibrium quantities: $y_1/y_2 > y_1^*/y_2^*$ ($*$ is the Foreign country)

Equilibrium prices: $p = p_1/p_2 < p^* = p_1^*/p_2^*$

---

## Trade equilibrium

**Claim**: since good 1 is L-intensive → $p^* > p$

If world price is $p$ then there will be an excess demand for good 1 in foreign country

Opening up to trade leads to an equilibrium world price

$$p < p^W < p^*$$

To see this let's construct the relative world demand

same preferences implies same rel demand in W as in H and F

rel. demand is all $p$'s such that

$$p = \frac{p_1}{p_2} = -\frac{U_2}{U_1} = p^* = \frac{p_1^*}{p_2^*} = -\frac{U_2^*}{U_1^*} = p^W$$

Relative supplies are $p$'s such that

$$p = \frac{p_1}{p_2} = -\frac{\partial h}{\partial y_1} = -\frac{\partial y_2}{\partial y_1} \text{ and } p^* = \frac{p_1^*}{p_2^*} = -\frac{\partial h^*}{\partial y_1^*} = -\frac{\partial y_2^*}{\partial y_1^*}$$

---

## Trade equilibrium

[Figure showing relative supply and demand curves intersecting]

Intuition: if H is abundant in L, when we open up to trade, the price of the L-intensive good goes up compared to its autarky level, opposite happens to the price of the other good. Why? → at the autarky price there is an excess demand for 1 coming from abroad (viceversa for the other good)

## The Heckscher-Ohlin theorem

### Theorem
*In a 2x2x2 economy, with same technologies, same homothetic preferences across countries, but different factor endowments, no FIR and both goods produced, the country relative abundant of labor exports the L-intensive good and the country relative rich of capital exports K-intensive goods.*

### Corollary (1)
*Trade increases welfare in both countries. Consumers reach a higher level of utility.*

### Corollary (2)
*Stolper-Samuelson: trade raises real reward of a country's abundant factor and reduces that of the scarce factor*

Trade leads to a **conflict of interest** between the scarce and abundant inputs.

### Corollary (3)
*Trade leads to factor price equalization*

## Trade and inequality

**Note**: there is only one factor of production in the Ricardian model → we can't use it to study distributional effects

HO is the textbook model to study distributional effects of trade

Assume that goods are produced with two types of labor: skilled and unskilled

HO model has predictions for effects of trade on wage inequality

Assume trade btw. a developed and a developing country: North-South trade

Assume: developed country is skill abundant, developing country unskill abundant

HO has 2 main predictions:

1. Trade increase relative price of skilled good in skill-intensive country
2. Trade increases inequality in skill-intensive country (developed) and decreases it in the unskill-intensive country (developing)
3. Trade increases inequality via across-sector reallocations of labor/production: from the scarce to the abundant sectors

The theory is beautiful, but is it useful? Let's check the data

## HO and the data: summing up

**HO prediction 1**: export prices in skilled-intensive sectors should increase compared to prices in unskill-intensive secs in skill abundant countries → not confirmed

**HO prediction 2**: inequality increase in skill-abundant countries and decreases in the others → not confirmed  

**HO prediction 3**: trade increases inequality via across-sector reallocation → not confirmed

Model propose an elegant a plausible explanation for recent increase in wage inequality

The basic version of the model produces a set of predictions that do not square with the data

There have been attempts to reconcile model with data: intermediate goods and offshoring (Feenstra, ch.4 ..... next year)

## Taking stock

- HO model explains trade using countries differences in **factor proportions**
- Elegant and intuitive theory of **trade patterns, gains from trade, and distributional effects**  
- Some problems matching the data
- Successful advanced applications combine HO features with other trade theories (e.g. Ricardian)
