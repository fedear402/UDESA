## Today: Learning by Exporting

▶ More productive firms export more. Melitz model provides a rational for this fact.

▶ It is quite establish that when firms become more productive, they are more likely to export.

▶ But can it be the other way round? Do exporting improve firm performance?

▶ This is know as "Learning by Exporting"

▶ **Papers**: 1) "Exporting and Firm Performance: Evidence from a Randomized Experiment", David Atkin, Amit K. Khandelwal, Adam Osman 2) "Importing after Exporting", Facundo Albornoz y Ezequiel Garcia Lembergman.

## Introduction to Learning by Exporting

▶ "Learning by Exporting" refers to the idea that firms can improve their productivity by entering foreign markets.

▶ Exposure to international competition and advanced technologies may lead to productivity improvements.

▶ Exporting firms gain access to:
  ▶ New knowledge and skills.
  ▶ Better managerial practices.
  ▶ Improved production techniques.

---

## Why Might Firms Learn by Exporting?

▶ Access to larger and more competitive markets:
  ▶ Forces firms to adopt better practices to survive.

▶ Technology spillovers:
  ▶ Exporting firms often interact with more advanced foreign firms and customers.
  ▶ Leads to knowledge transfer and innovation.

▶ Feedback from international customers:
  ▶ Allows firms to improve product quality and production processes.

---

## Empirical Evidence

▶ Several studies find evidence of productivity gains from exporting:
  ▶ Clerides, Lach, and Tybout (1998): Exporting leads to higher productivity in Colombia, Mexico, and Morocco.
  ▶ De Loecker (2007): Evidence from Slovenia shows exporting firms achieve higher productivity growth.
  ▶ Atkin et al, RCT in egypt
  ▶ Albornoz and Garcia-Lembergman: not only affects productivity, but also efficiency trough access to imported goods.

---

## Mechanisms of Learning by Exporting

▶ **Learning from customers**:
  ▶ Exporters often need to meet higher standards or specific requirements from foreign customers.

▶ **Increased competition**:
  ▶ Firms face stiffer competition in foreign markets, which forces them to innovate and improve efficiency.

▶ **Economies of scale**:
  ▶ Access to larger markets can help firms increase production and reduce average costs.

---

## Policy Implications

▶ Rational for encouraging firms to export can lead to long-term productivity gains.

▶ Governments may support firms through:
  ▶ Export promotion programs.
  ▶ Facilitating access to international markets.

▶ Policies should also address potential barriers to exporting, such as high entry costs or lack of information.

## EXPORTING AND FIRM PERFORMANCE:EVIDENCE FROM A RANDOM EXPERIMENT

**Authors: DAVID ATKIN, AMIT K. KHANDELWAL, ADAM OSMAN**

### Motivation

▶ Large productivity differences between countries (Hall and Jones 1999).

▶ Access to foreign markets is believed to help firms in developing countries close this gap.

▶ Key mechanism: Learning-by-exporting.

▶ The paper aims to provide causal evidence on the impact of exporting on firm performance through an RCT on rug producers in Egypt.

---

## Experiment Design of the RCT

▶ Randomized controlled trial with rug producers in Egypt.

▶ Intervention: Facilitated access to export markets for treatment firms.

▶ Control group: Firms not offered export opportunities.

▶ Export orders secured by partnering with a U.S.-based NGO and an Egyptian intermediary.

▶ Outcome measures: profits, productivity, and quality, tracked via surveys and product assessments.

---

## Random Assignment

▶ Sample: 303 rug producers from Fowa, Egypt.

▶ Randomized into treatment and control groups.

▶ Treatment group: Provided with export opportunities (e.g., orders for high-quality rugs).

▶ Control group: No export opportunities.

▶ Take-up: Firms in the treatment group that accepted export orders.

---

## Experiment design:

▶ Treatment firms were offered an initial export order with a price based on rug specifications.

▶ After the initial order the company could continue placing orders but was not required to make further purchases if firms failed to meet quality or timing standards.

▶ Control firms: Not contacted by company and excluded from exporting to this company throughout the experiment.

---

## Main Estimation

▶ Regression specification:

$$y_{it} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Treatment}_i + \gamma \cdot y_{i0} + \delta_s + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

▶ $y_{it}$: Outcome of interest (e.g., profits).

▶ $\text{Treatment}_i$: Indicator variable for firms in the treatment group.

▶ Controls for baseline outcome values, strata fixed effects, and time fixed effects.

## Main Results

▶ Exporting led to significant increases in firm profits (16-26% higher for treatment firms).

▶ Treatment firms produced higher-quality rugs compared to control firms.

▶ Learning effects were observed, with improvements in productivity and quality over time.

▶ No significant changes in production time, indicating efficiency improvements.

---

## Mechanisms Tested: Knowledge Transfer

▶ **Mechanism 1: Knowledge Transfer from Foreign Buyers**
  ▶ Treatment firms received feedback from foreign buyers through an intermediary.
  ▶ Findings:
    ▶ Quality improved most in dimensions discussed during feedback.
    ▶ Evidence of knowledge transfer from buyers to producers.

---

## Mechanisms Tested: Learning-by-doing and Efficiency

▶ **Mechanism 2: Learning-by-doing**
  ▶ Tracked productivity and quality over time to detect learning curves.
  ▶ Firms that exported over a longer period showed consistent improvement in both quality and productivity.
  ▶ Findings:
    ▶ Evidence of gradual learning-by-doing in treatment firms.
    ▶ Productivity gains were observed over time.

▶ **Mechanism 3: Efficiency Improvements**
  ▶ Tested productivity under controlled conditions (identical rug, same inputs, same loom)
  ▶ Findings:
    ▶ Evidence of efficiency gains.

---

## Conclusion

▶ The study provides reliable causal evidence of learning-by-exporting through an RCT.

▶ Exporting improves firm performance by increasing profits and product quality.

▶ Key mechanisms: knowledge transfer, learning-by-doing, and exposure to foreign market standards.

▶ Policy implications: Supporting firms' access to foreign markets can drive productivity improvements in developing countries.
